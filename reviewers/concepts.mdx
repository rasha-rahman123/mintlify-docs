## Fully generative vs. hybrid

A fully generative track is created end-to-end by a generator from a prompt — vocals, instruments, and mix all synthetic. A hybrid mixes human and AI elements, most commonly an AI vocal over a human instrumental.

Fully generative tracks usually fire on the full mix, because every part carries generation artifacts. Hybrids are the hard case and the reason per-stem analysis exists: the AI part of a hybrid can be a fraction of the audio, invisible to a whole-track average. The two produce visibly different results — basis `["full_mix"]` versus basis `["vocal"]` with a human [mix verdict](/reviewers/result-fields#mix-verdict).

## AI vocals: synthetic & converted

A synthetic vocal is generated outright. A voice conversion takes a real human performance and transforms its timbre into another voice — human timing and phrasing, synthetic sound.

Both count as AI vocals under the labeling standard. Conversions are the subtler listen: because the underlying performance is real, the synthetic character can surface only in moments — a tone that drifts toward the original voice mid-phrase. The [risk timeline](/reviewers/result-fields#risk-timeline) tells a reviewer where those moments are.

## Generator

A specific AI music service or model — Suno, Udio, Lyria, Treblo, Mureka, MusicGen, ElevenLabs. Each leaves a recognizably different acoustic signature, which is what makes origin attribution possible.

New generators appear continually. A track from one without an attribution reference still gets caught as AI; its origin simply reads `unknown` until a reference exists.

## False positives & false negatives

A false positive wrongly flags a human track as AI — the costliest error, because it accuses a real artist. A false negative lets an AI track through as human — costly downstream, when a streaming platform's own detection catches what was delivered to them.

The false-positive rate at each threshold is measured, not guessed: "one human track in a hundred" at the standard bar. No detector has zero of either error, which is why a person verifies every finding before it reaches an artist, and why catalogs get rescanned as engines improve.

## Genre blind spots

Detection error concentrates in specific genres and production styles rather than spreading evenly. Heavily produced, loudness-maximized styles are historically the hardest human material, because polish resembles the uniformity of generated audio.

Which genres run hot changes with every engine version, as training targets known gaps. This is why reporting the genre of every disputed flag matters: a review team that says "this flag was wrong, and the track is amapiano" is directly commissioning the fix.

<Tip>
**For reviewers** — when a flag lands on a genre known to run hot, weigh it accordingly, and send it back with the genre attached. That report is the retraining signal.
</Tip>

## The 2026 labeling standard

An industry agreement — RIAA, IFPI, A2IM, WIN, IMPALA and partners — defining when a recording is labeled AI-Generated versus AI-Assisted.

Its least intuitive rule is the one reviewers need most: a recording is AI-Generated when generative AI produced the entirety or the primary portion of its creative elements, and an AI lead vocal counts as a primary portion on its own. AI-Assisted is narrower than everyday usage — it requires a human lead vocal and human primary instruments, with AI only in secondary elements. A cloned voice over a human beat is AI-Generated under the standard, not AI-Assisted.

<Note>
Edge cases are still being clarified by the standard's authors.
</Note>
